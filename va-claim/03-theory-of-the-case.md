# Theory of the case

Plain-language legal framework so the owner, the VSO, and any agent are
arguing the same thing. Citations are to Title 38 (CFR / USC). A VSO or
accredited attorney checks this before filing; the law is stated as
understood on 2026-09-02.

## 1. What VA needs (the three elements of service connection, 38 CFR 3.303)

1. **A current disability** — a diagnosis made now, by a qualified clinician.
2. **An in-service event, injury, or disease** — here, the documented 2004
   psychiatric symptoms and the command-initiated psychiatric separation.
3. **A nexus** — a medical opinion that it is "at least as likely as not"
   (50% or better) that (1) is related to (2).

The standard of proof is benefit of the doubt (38 USC 5107(b)): a tie goes
to the veteran.

## 2. The personality-disorder problem, and why it is not fatal

- Personality disorders are "not diseases or injuries" for compensation
  (38 CFR 3.303(c), 4.9). A claim literally for "personality disorder" is
  denied as a matter of law. **Never claim it by that name.**
- But 38 CFR 4.127: "disability resulting from a mental disorder that is
  superimposed upon a personality disorder may be service-connected."
- And a claim for any mental condition is a claim for **whatever acquired
  psychiatric disorder the record reasonably raises** (Clemons v. Shinseki,
  23 Vet. App. 1 (2009)). VA must look past the label to the symptoms.
- The 2004 label is itself suspect on its face:
  - Personality disorders are by definition enduring patterns "of long
    duration". The soldier was 18, had a clean entrance exam, and the
    diagnosis came within months.
  - AR 635-200 para 5-13 (2004) required the diagnosis from a psychiatrist
    or a doctoral-level clinical psychologist, and a finding that the
    condition interfered with duty. The OMPF shows whether that was done.
  - DoD tightened PD-separation rules in 2008 after the GAO found the
    services had discharged tens of thousands of soldiers under 5-13 in
    2001–2007 with inadequate diagnostic support. This history is context
    for a clinician writing the nexus opinion and for ABCMR; it is not
    itself evidence.

**How the claim is framed:** "Acquired psychiatric disorder, to include
[current diagnosis — e.g. major depressive disorder / generalized anxiety
disorder / persistent adjustment disorder], with onset in service, previously
mischaracterized as personality disorder on separation in 2004."
Alternative theory in the same claim: acquired mental disorder superimposed
on a personality disorder (4.127).

## 3. Presumption of soundness (38 USC 1111; 38 CFR 3.304(b))

A veteran is presumed in sound condition at entry except for defects noted
on the entrance examination. The Persian Gulf War period (1990-08-02 to
present, 38 CFR 3.2(i)) is a period of war, so 1111 applies. To rebut, VA
must show by **clear and unmistakable evidence** BOTH that the condition
pre-existed service AND that it was not aggravated by service (Wagner v.
Principi, 370 F.3d 1089 (Fed. Cir. 2004)). If the separation packet claims
the disorder was "pre-existing", that claim, by itself, is not clear and
unmistakable evidence. If the entrance exam is clean, this presumption is
the spine of the case.

Action: get the DD 2807-1 / DD 2808 from the STRs. If any psychiatric history
WAS noted at MEPS, the theory shifts to aggravation (38 CFR 3.306) — still
viable, but tell the VSO immediately.

## 4. Continuity and the 22-year gap

VA will ask why the first claim comes in 2026. Answer it in the personal
statement, honestly: the discharge label carried stigma; the veteran built a
demanding career; symptoms were managed or unaddressed; he learned only
recently that a PD discharge does not bar a claim for the underlying
condition. A gap in treatment is not a bar (Buchanan v. Nicholson, 451 F.3d
1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006): lay evidence can establish continuity even without
contemporaneous medical records) — but it must be explained, and lay
statements from people who knew him before and after 2004 fill it.

## 5. Rating (38 CFR 4.130, General Rating Formula for Mental Disorders)

One rating covers ALL service-connected psychiatric diagnoses together.
Levels: 0 / 10 / 30 / 50 / 70 / 100 percent, keyed to **occupational and
social impairment**, not to the diagnosis. Rough anchors:

- 10%: mild symptoms, controlled by medication, decreased efficiency only
  under stress.
- 30%: occasional decrease in efficiency; depressed mood, anxiety, chronic
  sleep impairment, mild memory loss, panic attacks less than weekly.
- 50%: reduced reliability and productivity; panic attacks more than weekly,
  disturbances of motivation and mood, difficulty in work and social
  relationships.
- 70%: deficiencies in most areas (work, family, judgment, mood); suicidal
  ideation, near-continuous panic or depression, impaired impulse control,
  neglect of hygiene, inability to maintain relationships.
- 100%: total impairment.

The rater looks at frequency, severity, and duration of symptoms and their
effect on work and relationships (Vazquez-Claudio v. Shinseki, 713 F.3d 112
(Fed. Cir. 2013)). A high-functioning career does NOT preclude a rating; the
question is what it costs to function. The personal statement and the DBQ
are where this is documented — specifically, not generically.

Even a **0% (non-compensable) service connection** is a win for the owner's
stated goal: it is a "service-connected disability" for SDVOSB purposes
(13 CFR 128.200 defines service-disabled veteran by reference to 38 USC
101(16), no minimum percentage) and opens VA health-care priority and
future secondary claims.

## 6. Second lane: hearing / tinnitus (only if symptoms exist)

- 19K M1 Armor Crewman is "highly probable" noise exposure on VA's Duty MOS
  Noise Exposure Listing (M21-1). Basic-training weapons qualification adds
  to it. VA concedes exposure for the MOS; the questions become current
  diagnosis and nexus.
- Tinnitus: DC 6260, 10% maximum, lay-observable — the veteran's own
  credible report of ringing is competent evidence of the condition.
- Hearing loss: DC 6100, must meet 38 CFR 3.385 thresholds on a Maryland
  CNC / puretone audiogram. Get a VA audiology exam before deciding.
- Seven months of service is short; the exposure argument is real but the
  opinion must address it. Compare the entrance audiogram to a current one.

## 7. Other candidates (ask, do not assume)

Any injury in OSUT or at Fort Stewart (knees, back, ankles, feet from road
marches and tank work) documented in the STRs. Sleep disturbance and GI
symptoms are commonly secondary to a service-connected psychiatric
condition and are claimable later, after the primary condition is granted.

## 8. Risks and how they are handled

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| No current diagnosis | VA mental-health intake now (free, and VA records go straight into the claim file); or a private evaluation. Without this, do not file. |
| Separation packet says "pre-existing" | Presumption of soundness; clean MEPS exam; clinician addresses it head-on in the nexus opinion. |
| Examiner attributes everything to personality disorder | Nexus letter must explicitly discuss the 2004 diagnosis, explain why an acquired disorder is the better explanation (or is superimposed), and use the "at least as likely as not" language. |
| 22-year gap | Personal statement + buddy statements + any informal treatment history (PCP prescriptions, EAP). |
| Filing before evidence is in | Intent to File first; then file when the packet is complete. The ITF, not the 09-30 date, protects the money. |
| Paid "claim consultants" | Use only VA-accredited representatives (VSO, attorney, agent). Check accreditation at va.gov/ogc/apps/accreditation. Non-accredited firms charging a percentage are not permitted to represent claimants and add nothing a free VSO does not. |
| Discharge-related stigma being mis-argued | Character of service is already Honorable; no discharge upgrade is needed for VA eligibility. The ABCMR track is optional and separate. |
